# Evaluating GNN-Based Attacks on Logic-Locked Circuits

Research project exploring how structural transformations and alternative logic-locking schemes affect **GNNUnlock**, a graph-neural-network-based attack that classifies gates in a locked circuit as protection-logic (key/lock) gates or ordinary logic.

**Research collaboration:** Prof. Kyle Juretus  
**Tools:** Python, Perl, Verilog/bench netlists, GraphSAINT, PyTorch, NumPy, SciPy, NetworkX, Matplotlib, Google Colab, Terminal, Git

## Overview

Logic locking adds key-controlled logic to a circuit to protect its intellectual property. This project evaluates whether a GNN can identify the added protection logic from a circuit's structure, and whether changing that structure makes gate identification less reliable.

The experiments build on the open-source [GNNUnlock implementation](https://github.com/DfX-NYUAD/GNNUnlock) and its graph-parsing/training workflow. The notebooks investigate:

- **Baseline gate identification** on Anti-SAT-locked ISCAS benchmark circuits.
- **Structural defenses**, including De Morgan transformations, wire insertion, gate camouflage, and random structural perturbations.
- **XOR/XNOR decomposition**, replacing key-related XOR/XNOR gates with equivalent AND/OR/NOT subgraphs.
- **CAC (Corrupt-and-Correct) locking**, including tests on circuits from the [Valkyrie CAC dataset](https://github.com/LL-Tools/Valkyrie/tree/main/CAC).
- **Gate-level analysis and visualization**, including prediction confidence, class accuracy, and circuit subgraphs.

The work is experimental here.

### Verdict Thresholds

| Attack Accuracy | Classification | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Above 65% | **Vulnerable** | Attack performs substantially above chance |
| 55–65% | **Partial** | Some predictive capability |
| 50–55% | **Borderline** | Attack performs only slightly above chance |
| Below 50% | **Defended** | Below-chance performance under this metric |

**Key takeaway:** All tested transformations remained in the borderline range, with accuracy between 50.7% and 52.8%. Cone restructuring produced the largest reported reduction, while wire splitting slightly increased accuracy. Because the baseline attack itself performed near chance, these results are not as conclusive, but they show some guessing scheme, and establish that the transformations successfully defended against a properly formulated key-gate-localization attack in some way, although this is not as conclusive as AntiSAT.


These CAC results are near chance-level for a two-class classification task, according to the report's own interpretation. They are separate from the Anti-SAT results above and should not be combined into one headline accuracy.

## Experimental Workflow

At a high level, the notebooks follow this workflow:

1. **Prepare circuit data.** Use Anti-SAT/ISCAS `.bench` circuits or CAC-locked Verilog circuits as inputs.
2. **Apply a structural transformation.** Depending on the experiment, preserve the raw circuit or generate a transformed variant.
3. **Parse the netlist.** Use the GNNUnlock Perl parser and graph-processing scripts to represent circuit gates and connections as graph data.
4. **Train and evaluate the GNN.** Run the GraphSAINT node-classification workflow and collect gate-level predictions.
5. **Measure results.** Compare predicted gate classes with labels and report accuracy and, in some notebooks, confidence scores or class-level metrics.
6. **Inspect graph structure.** Use NetworkX and Matplotlib to visualize circuits or regions around predicted lock gates.

Exact steps, training splits, label conventions, and output paths vary between notebooks. In particular, some notebooks patch upstream training/parser code and some contain multiple alternative implementations.

## Key Findings and Conclusions

- **Anti-SAT circuits remained highly vulnerable to GNN-based analysis.** GNNUnlock achieved high gate-identification accuracy, often in the high 90s or reaching 100%, even when evaluated on structurally transformed circuits without retraining on those transformations.

- **Structural transformations had varying effectiveness.** De Morgan transformations, wire insertion, XOR/XNOR decomposition, and random structural perturbations generally failed to substantially reduce gate-identification accuracy in the tested Anti-SAT experiments. Gate-level confidence scores also helped identify which predictions were certain versus ambiguous.

- **Gate camouflage did not eliminate recognizable structural patterns.** Decomposing XOR/XNOR gates into equivalent logic structures introduced uncertainty in some predictions, particularly around transformed AND/OR gates, but the attack continued to identify most gates correctly.

- **CAC experiments produced inconclusive defense comparisons.** A custom GraphSAGE-based attack I built sourced from the Valkyrie Repo (due to license tools to run the true Valkyrie attack, I could not get it) achieved approximately 52.7% baseline accuracy on the tested CAC circuit, near chance level for binary classification. I had to use the Valkyrie repository as a Investigation revealed that the initial approach incorrectly attempted to predict key values from wire names rather than localizing key-related gates. Consequently, the CAC transformation results should be treated as preliminary rather than evidence that the defenses are effective.

**Overall takeaway:** The experiments suggest that logical and even drastic structural transformations alone may be insufficient to conceal Anti-SAT locking patterns from GNN-based gate-identification attacks. However, further research can be done to establish reproducibility and evaluate defenses across different locking schemes and circuit datasets, especially with CAC-lockes.
